\abstract{%
Mining high-utility itemsets from real-world data often meets three
challenges at once: the analyst cannot set a sensible minimum-utility
threshold in advance (top-$\Kk$ mining), the data is noisy or aggregated
and each entry carries an existence probability (uncertain databases),
and some items represent losses rather than gains (negative profits).
Together these define the Top-$\Kk$ Probabilistic Positive--Negative
High-Utility Itemset (PPNHUI) problem. Negative profits invalidate
utility-based pruning and enlarge the search, which makes parallel
execution attractive; but in top-$\Kk$ mining the admission threshold is
discovered during the search, so a worker can either help its peers by
raising it early or waste effort on branches a peer has already made
redundant. We give an exact mining core based on a utility--probability
list (UPU-List) with three upper bounds, proven valid under negative
profits and under both a discovered and a supplied threshold, and use it
to study four classical shared-memory parallelisation schemes that differ
along two axes: threshold sharing and load balancing. Two of the four
differ in exactly one variable, which makes their gap a controlled
measurement of what sharing is worth, and we add a sequential
reverse-order control that isolates the order in which prefixes are
enumerated from the parallel machinery. The control changes the
conclusion. A work-stealing scheduler appears to prune aggressively, but
on one thread it attempts exactly the same list joins as the sequential
control---the same count, to the digit, on every dataset and every
$\Kk$---and matches its runtime to within a few percent, and at eight
threads its work ratio still tracks that control: its pruning follows from the order
its task decomposition happens to impose, not from cooperation between
workers. That order is a gamble rather than a gain---it cuts the work to
$0.53\times$ on one dataset and raises it to $2.34\times$ on another---and
the scheduler inherits the loss as readily as the win. Threshold sharing,
measured on the controlled pair instead, is the mechanism that works: it
saves between $1.12\times$ and $3.01\times$ of the exploration on every
dataset, dense and sparse alike, and it is precisely what the
partition-local pattern used throughout the parallel HUIM literature
forgoes. A producer--consumer scheduler, which shares the threshold and
imposes no order of its own, is the best or joint-best choice on eight of
the nine datasets. All of these effects vanish under a supplied
threshold, where the work ratio is exactly $1.000$ in every configuration
measured, so the design question studied here belongs to top-$\Kk$ mining
rather than to parallel utility mining in general. The core is validated
itemset by itemset against an independent published exact miner on a
reduced base case, where it is also faster. We close with a diagnostic
that needs one sequential measurement rather than a guess from data
statistics.
}

\keywords{high-utility itemset mining, uncertain databases, top-$\Kk$ mining,
negative utility, parallel algorithms}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
High-utility itemset mining (HUIM) generalises frequent itemset mining by
weighting items with utilities such as profit, revealing patterns that are
valuable rather than merely frequent~\cite{liu2012huiminer,gan2021survey}. Three
extensions move HUIM closer to real applications. First, the analyst rarely
knows a good minimum-utility threshold; \emph{top-$\Kk$} mining instead asks
directly for the $\Kk$ itemsets of highest utility~\cite{wu2012topk,tseng2016topk}.
Second, data gathered from sensors, imputation, or aggregation is
\emph{uncertain}: each item in a transaction carries an existence
probability~\cite{lin2016phuim}. Third, real profit tables contain
\emph{negative} entries---loss leaders, penalties, adverse events---so a faithful
model must allow items with negative utility~\cite{chu2009huiniv,lin2016fhn}.

Combining all three yields the Top-$\Kk$ Probabilistic Positive--Negative
High-Utility Itemset (PPNHUI) problem. Negative items prevent the search from
being pruned by utility alone and so enlarge it, which motivates parallel
execution. However, whether parallelism actually helps is subtle. Top-$\Kk$
algorithms prune using an admission threshold that equals the $\Kk$-th best utility
found \emph{so far}; this threshold starts low and rises only as good itemsets
are discovered~\cite{tseng2016topk}. In a parallel execution many branches are
explored before the threshold has risen, creating redundant work; conversely, if
several workers \emph{share} the threshold, an early discovery by one worker can
prune the others. Which effect dominates is data-dependent, and negative items,
by depressing utilities, slow the threshold's ascent. A study of parallel PPNHUI
mining therefore needs a metric that separates algorithmic pruning from raw
hardware speedup.

This paper provides such a study for the \emph{shared-memory} setting, where the
exact, instantaneous sharing of a global threshold is possible---something a
distributed engine cannot easily offer. Our contributions are:


\begin{enumerate}
\item We formalise the Top-$\Kk$ PPNHUI problem over uncertain databases
      together with its fixed-threshold counterpart
      (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}), and give a mining core built on a
      utility--probability list with three upper bounds
      (Section~\ref{sec:methods}). We prove that all three remain valid
      when item profits may be negative, and that the search is exact
      whenever the admission threshold never exceeds the qualifying
      utility of the answer---a condition that covers both problems and
      that also establishes the safety of sharing the threshold between
      workers (Lemmas~\ref{lem:ep}--\ref{lem:pub},
      Theorem~\ref{thm:correct}).
 
\item We validate that core outside our own code. Reduced to its
      classical special case it is compared, itemset by itemset and
      utility by utility, with an independently written published exact
      miner on the same data (Section~\ref{subsec:basecase}), so the
      claim of exactness does not rest on agreement with our own
      sequential baseline.
 
\item We introduce a control that the parallel mining literature does
      not use: a sequential miner differing from the baseline only in the
      direction of its top-level loop (Section~\ref{subsec:seqrev}). It
      separates the pruning a scheduler obtains from cooperation between
      its workers from the pruning it obtains merely by visiting prefixes
      in a different order. We show that without this control the second
      is routinely mistaken for the first.
 
\item We measure what sharing the admission threshold is worth, using
      two strategies that share an identical prefix distribution and an
      identical growth procedure and differ only in whether the collector
      is shared (Section~\ref{subsec:sharing}). This isolates the
      mechanism from load balancing, which the usual comparison between
      parallel schemes confounds with it.
 
\item We evaluate the four schemes on nine benchmarks spanning three
      orders of magnitude of density, at one, two, four and eight
      threads, and we delimit the findings by repeating the study under
      a supplied threshold, where the mechanisms under examination no
      longer act (Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:discussion}). We
      conclude with a selection diagnostic that a practitioner can
      measure with a single sequential run.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Top-$\Kk$ HUIM.}
Top-$\Kk$ HUIM removes the minimum-utility threshold. Two-phase methods such as
TKU~\cite{wu2012topk} and REPT~\cite{ryang2015rept} first generate candidates and
then verify them, while one-phase methods such as TKO~\cite{tseng2016topk} and
KHMC~\cite{duong2016khmc} use a vertical utility-list~\cite{liu2012huiminer} and
raise the threshold during a single traversal. All of these target
\emph{certain} databases with non-negative utilities. Top-$\Kk$ mining remains
active: recent advances include a metaheuristic top-$\Kk$ miner
(TKU-BChOA~\cite{mofid2024tku}), threshold-raising with the pre-large concept on
incremental data~\cite{tung2025incremental}, incremental top-$\Kk$ mining over an
accumulated database~\cite{lee2024incremental}, top-$\Kk$ cross-level mining over
data streams~\cite{han2024crosslevel}, and join-free utility-list
traversal~\cite{yan2024nojoin}.

\paragraph{HUIM over uncertain databases.}
Lin et al.~\cite{lin2016phuim} introduced potential high-utility itemset mining
(PHUIM) for uncertain data, with the level-wise PHUI-UP and the list-based
PHUI-List built on a probability-utility (PU) list; MUHUI~\cite{lin2017muhui}
adds pruning to the same structure. These methods mine \emph{all} potential
high-utility itemsets rather than a top-$\Kk$ set, and assume non-negative profits.
Closest to our setting, Nguyen and Nguyen~\cite{nguyen2025dynamic}
attack the top-$\Kk$ problem over uncertain data by letting both the
probability and the utility admission thresholds tighten as mining
proceeds, so that early discoveries restrict the remaining search.
Their setting keeps profits non-negative and their algorithm is
sequential, so the question of how several workers should share a
rising threshold---the subject of this paper---does not arise there.


\paragraph{HUIM with negative utilities.}
HUINIV-Mine~\cite{chu2009huiniv} first handled negative item values;
FHN~\cite{lin2016fhn} introduced a positive-and-negative utility list
(PNU-list) that is far faster, and surveys document the
area~\cite{singh2018negsurvey}. Negative profits have since been
combined with top-$\Kk$ selection in the \emph{certain} setting: TKN
mines the top-$\Kk$ itemsets under positive or negative
profits~\cite{krishnamoorthy2022tkn}, TopHUI addresses the same problem
with a different bound~\cite{gan2020tophui}, and TKHN improves the
threshold-raising strategies for it~\cite{singh2026tkhn}. Related
variants mine negatives from unstable negative-profit
databases~\cite{tung2024unstable} and from high-utility sequences with
positive and negative values~\cite{zhang2023posneg}. All of these assume
certain data and are sequential. Our UPU-List combines the PU-list idea
of~\cite{lin2016phuim} with the positive/negative bookkeeping
of~\cite{lin2016fhn}; the structure itself is therefore evolutionary
rather than novel, which is why our contribution is the parallel study
and its attribution methodology, not the data structure.

\paragraph{Parallel and distributed HUIM.}
Big-data HUIM has largely been distributed: PHUI-Growth on
Hadoop~\cite{lin2015bigdata}, PKU for parallel top-$\Kk$ HUIM on
Spark~\cite{lin2019pku}, P-FHM+ on Spark~\cite{sethi2018pfhm},
Hadoop-based P-EFIM~\cite{cheng2023hadoop}, and more recent Spark-based
parallel HUIM~\cite{li2024spark}. These frameworks partition the work
into largely independent subtasks and therefore cannot share an exact
global threshold across nodes. A separate line of work accelerates HUIM
on GPUs using population-based heuristics, of which PHA-HUIM is the most
recent representative~\cite{fang2024gpu}.
 
We deliberately do not report a head-to-head runtime comparison against
either family, for reasons that are properties of the problems being
solved rather than of any particular implementation. The GPU heuristics
return \emph{approximate} answers: PHA-HUIM evolves a fixed-size
population under a utility fitness function and reports the high-utility
itemsets its population happens to contain when the iteration budget is
exhausted, with no guarantee of completeness. Our method is exact
(Theorem~\ref{thm:correct}), and a wall-clock comparison between an
exact and an approximate search is uninterpretable unless solution
quality is controlled, since an approximate method can always be made
faster by searching less. The two also differ in hardware---massively
parallel GPU versus commodity multi-core CPU---so a runtime ratio would
measure the hardware rather than the algorithm; and in the query itself,
since the GPU heuristics mine at a fixed minimum-utility threshold
whereas we solve the top-$\Kk$ problem, in which the threshold is
discovered during the search and is precisely what the parallelisation
strategies contend over. The distributed frameworks differ from our
setting along that same last axis: they cannot maintain the exact shared
threshold whose dynamics are the subject of this study. We therefore
validate against an exact reference instead:
Section~\ref{subsec:basecase} reduces our algorithm to the classical
HUIM special case and checks it, itemset by itemset, against EFIM as
distributed in SPMF~\cite{fournierviger2014spmf,zida2017efim}---an
independently written, published, exact miner that shares no code, data
structure or author with our implementation.

\paragraph{Shared-memory parallel HUIM.}
Multi-core HUIM has attracted less attention than the distributed
setting, but it is not unexplored. Atmaja and Sonawane parallelise a utility-list buffer miner over shared memory, dividing the search space across threads~\cite{atmaja2023smplb}.
Most relevant to us, Tung et al.\ mine top-$\Kk$ multi-level high-utility
itemsets with multi-core parallelism~\cite{tung2026multicore}, the
closest prior combination of top-$\Kk$ selection with shared-memory
parallel execution. That work parallelises a multi-level mining
task over certain, non-negative data and reports wall-clock speedup;
it does not separate the pruning that threshold sharing produces
from the parallelism that produces it, which is the question we study.
To our knowledge no prior work examines how the \emph{choice} of
shared-memory parallelisation strategy interacts with top-$\Kk$
threshold dynamics under uncertain, positive--negative utilities, nor
isolates the enumeration-order component of the resulting speedup. That
is the gap this paper addresses, and Table~\ref{tab:litcompare} places
our setting among the closest prior work.

\section{Preliminaries and Problem Statement}\label{sec:prelim}
\subsection{Basic Concepts}\label{subsec:defs}
Let $I=\{i_1,\dots,i_m\}$ be a set of items. A \emph{profit table} assigns each
$i\in I$ a value $\pr(i)\in\mathbb{R}$; unlike classical HUIM, $\pr(i)$ may be
negative.

\begin{definition}[Uncertain transaction database]\label{def:db}
$\DB=\{T_1,\dots,T_n\}$ where each transaction $T_d$ associates with every item
$i\in T_d$ a quantity $\qty(i,T_d)\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}$ and an existence probability
$\prob(i,T_d)\in(0,1]$. Item probabilities are assumed independent.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Utility]\label{def:util}
$\uu(i,T)=\pr(i)\,\qty(i,T)$ and, for $\itemset\subseteq T$,
$\uu(\itemset,T)=\sum_{i\in \itemset}\pr(i)\,\qty(i,T)$, which may be
negative.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Probabilities]\label{def:prob}
$\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T)=\prod_{i\in \itemset}\prob(i,T)$ for $\itemset\subseteq T$
(and $0$ otherwise). The \emph{expected utility} and \emph{existential
probability} of $\itemset$ are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eu}
\EU(\itemset)=\!\!\sum_{T\supseteq \itemset}\!\!\uu(\itemset,T)\,\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T),
\qquad
\EP(\itemset)=1-\!\!\prod_{T\supseteq \itemset}\!\!\bigl(1-\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Because $\uu(\itemset,T)$ may be negative, $\EU(\itemset)$ may be negative.
\end{definition}

\begin{problem}[Top-$\Kk$ PPNHUI mining]\label{prob:main}
Throughout, itemsets are non-empty. Given $\DB$, a profit table $\pr$,
$\Kk\ge 1$ and $\minprob\in[0,1]$, let
$\mathcal{V}=\{\itemset\subseteq I:\EP(\itemset)\ge\minprob\}$.
Return $\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{V}$ with
$|\mathcal{R}|=\min(\Kk,|\mathcal{V}|)$ such that
$\EU(\itemset)\ge\EU(Y)$ for every $\itemset\in\mathcal{R}$ and
$Y\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{R}$. Setting $\minprob=0$ admits every
itemset that occurs at all, which recovers the top-$\Kk$ problem without
a probabilistic filter.
\end{problem}


\begin{problem}[Fixed-threshold PPNHUI mining]\label{prob:minutil}
Given $\DB$, a profit table $\pr$, a threshold $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ and
$\minprob\in[0,1]$, return
$\{\itemset\subseteq I:\EP(\itemset)\ge\minprob,\
\EU(\itemset)\ge\mu\}$, that is, \emph{every} valid itemset whose
expected utility reaches $\mu$.
\end{problem}
 
\begin{remark}[One core, two questions]\label{rem:twomodes}
Problems~\ref{prob:main} and~\ref{prob:minutil} differ only in where the
admission threshold $\tau$ comes from. In Problem~\ref{prob:main} it is
\emph{discovered}: it starts at $-\infty$ and rises to the $\Kk$-th best
$\EU$ found so far. In Problem~\ref{prob:minutil} it is \emph{supplied}
and constant, $\tau=\mu$. Everything else---the UPU-List, the join, and
the three pruning tiers---is identical, so a single implementation
answers both, and Theorem~\ref{thm:correct} covers both because its
proof requires only that $\tau$ never exceed the final answer's
qualifying utility, which a constant $\tau=\mu$ satisfies trivially.
This is not a mere convenience: Section~\ref{subsec:basecase} uses the
fixed-threshold mode to compare against an external exact miner, and
finds that the dynamics this paper studies exist only when $\tau$ is
discovered.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Negative-utility itemsets]\label{rem:negEU}
Problem~\ref{prob:main} places no sign constraint on $\EU$: when fewer than $\Kk$
valid itemsets have non-negative expected utility, the least-negative ones are
returned. This matches the positive--negative setting and our implementation. If
an application requires $\EU\ge0$, one adds that constraint to $\mathcal{V}$ and
lower-bounds the admission threshold at $0$, which is Problem~\ref{prob:minutil} with $\mu=0$ intersected with a top-$\Kk$ cut.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Illustrative Example}\label{subsec:example}

Table~\ref{tab:exampledb} shows a small uncertain database over items
$\{a,b,c\}$ with profits $\pr(a)=5$, $\pr(b)=-3$, $\pr(c)=2$ (so $b$ is
a negative-profit item); each entry lists an item's quantity and
existence probability. Applying Definition~\ref{def:prob},
Table~\ref{tab:exampleeu} gives $\EU$ and $\EP$ for every itemset. With
$\minprob=0.7$ all seven non-empty itemsets are valid, and ranking them
by $\EU$ gives
$\{a\}\!>\!\{a,c\}\!>\!\{c\}\!>\!\{a,b\}\!>\!\{a,b,c\}\!>\!\{b,c\}\!>\!\{b\}$.
 
The ranking illustrates Remark~\ref{rem:negEU} concretely. For
$\Kk\le5$ the answer contains only positive-$\EU$ itemsets, even though
two of them---$\{a,b\}$ and $\{a,b,c\}$---contain the negative-profit
item $b$: a negative item does not make an itemset unprofitable, it
only subtracts from it. At $\Kk=6$ the answer must admit
$\{b,c\}$ ($\EU=-2.75$), and at $\Kk=7$ also $\{b\}$ ($\EU=-9.3$),
because no other valid itemset remains. A top-$\Kk$ miner that refused
negative-$\EU$ patterns would return fewer than $\Kk$ answers here and
would therefore not solve Problem~\ref{prob:main}.
 
Two further points are worth noting. First, $\EP(\{b\})=1$ because $b$
occurs with probability $1$ in transaction $T_4$; an item with a
negative profit can still be certain to occur. Second, every row
satisfies $\EU\le\PTWU$, as Lemma~\ref{lem:ptwu} requires, and the
slack is large---$\{a\}$ has $\EU=14.50$ against $\PTWU=28$---which is
why the $\PTWU$ tier alone prunes far less than the three tiers
together.

\section{Proposed Methods}\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Mining Core and Upper Bounds}\label{subsec:core}
The sequential baseline and the four parallel strategies share one mining core
and differ only in how Phase~3 is
parallelised (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Phase~1 scans $\DB$ once to compute per-item bounds and probabilities, discards items that cannot belong to any valid pattern, and builds a UPU-List per surviving item. Phase~2 seeds the result with the valid
$1$-itemsets. Phase~3 is a depth-first traversal of the set-enumeration tree that
extends each prefix by joining UPU-Lists, applying the three bounds at
four points. On entering a node the prefix's own $\PTWU$ is tested,
which abandons an entire subtree for the cost of one comparison. Inside
\textsc{Join} the bound $\PTWU(Z)=\min(\PTWU(\itemset),\PTWU(e))$ is
available \emph{before} any transaction is examined, so a doomed
extension is rejected without paying for the merge at all; this
pre-merge test is the cheapest pruning in the algorithm and, on dense
data, the one that fires most often. Only when the merge does happen are
the remaining tests applied to its result, in the order $\EP$, $\PTWU$,
$\PUB$ --- cheapest first, since $\EP$ and $\PTWU$ are single cached
values whereas $\PUB$ was accumulated during the merge. The sequence is
therefore $\PTWU$ at the node, $\PTWU$ before the merge, then $\EP$,
$\PTWU$ and $\PUB$ on the result, rather than the flat
$\EP\to\PTWU\to\PUB$ ordering that the phrase ``three tiers'' might
suggest.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_pipeline.pdf}
\caption{Overview of the mining pipeline. The three-phase core (build, seed,
prune-and-grow) is shared by all methods; only Phase~3---the depth-first
pattern-growth traversal---is parallelised, and the four strategies differ in how
they share the admission threshold $\tau$ and balance prefix
tasks.}\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

For an itemset $\itemset$, the UPU-List stores, per transaction
$T\supseteq \itemset$, the utility $\uu(\itemset,T)$, a positive remaining utility
$\rem(\itemset,T)$, and $\log\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T)$ (log-space for stability);
two lists are joined by a linear two-pointer merge (Figure~\ref{fig:upulist}).
Concretely, to extend a prefix $\itemset$ by an item $e$ (giving $Z=\itemset\cup\{e\}$),
the merge keeps only transactions $t$ present in both lists and, on each such
matching $t$, combines the entries by
\begin{align}
\uu(Z,t)&=\uu(\itemset,t)+\uu(e,t), &
\rem(Z,t)&=\min\!\bigl(\rem(\itemset,t),\rem(e,t)\bigr), \nonumber\\
\log\mathrm{P}(Z,t)&=\log\mathrm{P}(\itemset,t)+\log\mathrm{P}(e,t).
\label{eq:joinrow}
\end{align}
During the same pass the four cached aggregates are accumulated as
\begin{gather}
\EU(Z)=\sum_{t} \uu(Z,t)\,e^{\log\mathrm{P}(Z,t)}, \qquad
\PTWU(Z)=\min\!\bigl(\PTWU(\itemset),\PTWU(e)\bigr), \label{eq:joineu}\\
\PUB(Z)=\!\!\sum_{\substack{t:\ \uu(Z,t)+\rem(Z,t)>0}}\!\!
        e^{\log\mathrm{P}(Z,t)}\bigl(\uu(Z,t)+\rem(Z,t)\bigr), \label{eq:joinpub}\\
\EP(Z)=1-\prod_{t}\bigl(1-e^{\log\mathrm{P}(Z,t)}\bigr). \label{eq:joinep}
\end{gather}
where $\EP(Z)$ is accumulated in log-space for numerical stability. These are
exactly the operations annotated in Figure~\ref{fig:upulist}. The bound cached in a joined list is $\min(\PTWU(\itemset),\PTWU(e))$, which
is valid but possibly looser than the sum $\sum_{T\supseteq Z}\PTU(T)$
defined below, since $\{T\supseteq Z\}$ is a subset of both occurrence sets;
Lemma~\ref{lem:ptwu} holds for either.
Fix a total order $\prec$ on
items (descending $\PTWU$). With $\PTU(T)=\sum_{i\in T,\pr(i)>0}\pr(i)\qty(i,T)$,

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_upulist.pdf}
\caption{The UPU-List structure and the two-pointer \textsc{Join}. Entries sharing
a transaction id are merged and the cached aggregates $\EU$, $\EP$, $\PTWU$ and
$\PUB$ are updated; the merge and aggregation rules are given in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:joinrow}--\eqref{eq:joinep}.}\label{fig:upulist}
\end{figure}
\begin{gather}
\PTWU(\itemset)=\sum_{T\supseteq \itemset}\PTU(T), \qquad
\rem(\itemset,T)=\sum_{\substack{i\in T,\ i\succ \itemset,\ \pr(i)>0}}\pr(i)\qty(i,T), \label{eq:ptwurem}\\
\PUB(\itemset)=\sum_{\substack{T\supseteq \itemset:\ \uu(\itemset,T)+\rem(\itemset,T)>0}}\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T)\bigl(\uu(\itemset,T)+\rem(\itemset,T)\bigr). \label{eq:pub}
\end{gather}

Throughout the listings, $\epsilon=10^{-9}$ is the tolerance with which
floating-point comparisons against a threshold are made, and
$\textsc{LogZero}=-700$ is the floor of the log-space probability
accumulator, chosen so that $e^{\textsc{LogZero}}$ is indistinguishable
from $0$ in double precision.

An \emph{extension} of $\itemset$ is any $Z\supseteq \itemset$ whose extra items
follow $\itemset$ under $\prec$.

\begin{lemma}[$\EP$ anti-monotonicity]\label{lem:ep}
If $\itemset\subseteq Z$ then $\EP(Z)\le\EP(\itemset)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\mathrm{P}(Z,T)\le\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T)$ as each $\prob(i,T)\in(0,1]$, so
$1-\mathrm{P}(Z,T)\ge1-\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T)$ for all $T$ and the product over
$T$ is no smaller for $Z$; hence $\EP(Z)\le\EP(\itemset)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[$\PTWU$ bound]\label{lem:ptwu}
$\EU(Z)\le\PTWU(Z)\le\PTWU(\itemset)$ for any $\itemset\subseteq Z$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $T\supseteq Z$, $\uu(Z,T)\mathrm{P}(Z,T)\le\max\{\uu(Z,T),0\}\le\PTU(T)$:
if $\uu(Z,T)\ge0$ then multiplying by $\mathrm{P}(Z,T)\in(0,1]$ does not increase
it, otherwise the left side is negative; and the positive part of $\uu(Z,T)$ is
at most the positive utilities of $T$. Summing over $T\supseteq Z$ gives
$\EU(Z)\le\PTWU(Z)$. Since $\{T\supseteq Z\}\subseteq\{T\supseteq \itemset\}$ and
$\PTU\ge0$, $\PTWU(Z)\le\PTWU(\itemset)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[$\PUB$ bound]\label{lem:pub}
For every extension $Z$ of $\itemset$, $\EU(Z)\le\PUB(\itemset)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $T\supseteq Z$ the extra items of $Z$ lie in the $\prec$-suffix, so their
positive contribution is at most $\rem(\itemset,T)$ and
$\uu(Z,T)\le\uu(\itemset,T)+\rem(\itemset,T)$; also
$\mathrm{P}(Z,T)\le\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:ep}. Hence
$\uu(Z,T)\mathrm{P}(Z,T)\le\max\{0,\uu(\itemset,T)+\rem(\itemset,T)\}\,\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T)$.
Summing over $T\supseteq Z$ and dropping the non-positive terms gives
$\EU(Z)\le\PUB(\itemset)$.
\end{proof}


\begin{theorem}[Correctness]\label{thm:correct}
Let $\tau$ be any admission threshold that never exceeds the qualifying
utility of the answer---that is, the final $\Kk$-th largest $\EU$ for
Problem~\ref{prob:main}, or the constant $\mu$ for
Problem~\ref{prob:minutil}. Pruning $\itemset$ when
$\EP(\itemset)<\minprob$, $\PTWU(\itemset)<\tau$ or $\PUB(\itemset)<\tau$
never discards an itemset of the answer. Hence the search is exact for
both problems.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:ep}, if $\EP(\itemset)<\minprob$ then
$\EP(Z)\le\EP(\itemset)<\minprob$ for every $Z\supseteq\itemset$, so
$\itemset$ and all its extensions are invalid and none belongs to the
answer. By Lemmas~\ref{lem:ptwu}--\ref{lem:pub}, if
$\PTWU(\itemset)<\tau$ or $\PUB(\itemset)<\tau$ then $\EU(Z)<\tau$ for
every extension $Z$ of $\itemset$. Since $\tau$ never exceeds the
qualifying utility, $\EU(Z)<\tau$ implies $Z$ does not qualify: for
Problem~\ref{prob:minutil} because $\tau=\mu$ and $\EU(Z)<\mu$
directly; for Problem~\ref{prob:main} because $\tau$ is at all times a
lower bound on the final $\Kk$-th best $\EU$, so $Z$ cannot displace a
member of the final top-$\Kk$. In both cases only subtrees lying
entirely below the qualifying utility are pruned.
 
The top-$\Kk$ collector satisfies the hypothesis: $\tau$ starts at
$-\infty$, is updated only to the $\Kk$-th largest $\EU$ admitted so
far, and that quantity increases monotonically towards, but never past,
the final $\Kk$-th best. The fixed-threshold collector satisfies it
trivially with $\tau=\mu$ throughout. The hypothesis is also what makes
threshold \emph{sharing} safe: a thread-local threshold is a smaller
admissible $\tau$, and the global one is the largest admissible $\tau$
available at any instant, so sharing prunes more without ever
endangering a member of the answer.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why sharing helps and why negatives matter]\label{rem:sharing}
Theorem~\ref{thm:correct} holds for any $\tau$ not exceeding the final $\Kk$-th best
$\EU$. Raising $\tau$ earlier---as when many workers feed a shared
collector---prunes more without ever discarding a solution; a thread-local
threshold is simply a smaller, also-safe $\tau$ that prunes less. Negative items
depress $\EU$ and slow the ascent of $\tau$, which both reduces the benefit of
sharing and increases redundant parallel exploration.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Pattern Growth}\label{subsec:dfs}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:dfs} is the shared growth procedure; $\tau$ is read from a
top-$\Kk$ collector holding the current $\Kk$ highest-$\EU$ itemsets.


\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Grow}: depth-first pattern growth from a prefix}\label{alg:dfs}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require prefix $P$, start index $s$, ordered items $\sigma$, $1$-itemset lists $\mathcal{L}$, collector $C$, $\minprob$
\If{$P.\PTWU < C.\textsc{threshold}() - \epsilon$} \Return \EndIf \Comment{$\PTWU$ at the node}
\For{$j \gets s$ \textbf{to} $|\sigma|-1$}
  \If{$\sigma[j] \notin \mathcal{L}$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \State $J \gets \textsc{Join}(P,\ \mathcal{L}[\sigma[j]],\ C.\textsc{threshold}())$
  \If{$J=\textsc{null}$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf \Comment{rejected before the merge}
  \If{$J.\EP  < \minprob                    - \epsilon$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \If{$J.\PTWU < C.\textsc{threshold}() - \epsilon$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \If{$J.\PUB  < C.\textsc{threshold}() - \epsilon$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \If{$J.\EU  \ge C.\textsc{threshold}() - \epsilon$} $C.\textsc{tryCollect}(J)$ \EndIf
  \State \textsc{Grow}($J,\ j{+}1,\ \sigma,\ \mathcal{L},\ C,\ \minprob$)
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
 
Two details of Algorithm~\ref{alg:dfs} are deliberate and matter for the
results. First, the threshold is \emph{re-read} from the collector at
every test rather than cached once per node. Under the shared-collector
strategies this is what lets a discovery made by one worker prune
another worker's branch part-way through a prefix; caching it would
still be correct, but would describe a weaker form of sharing and would
not reproduce the work ratios of Section~\ref{sec:results}. Second, all
comparisons carry a tolerance $\epsilon=10^{-9}$. Since $\EU$ is
accumulated in floating point through an exponential, an itemset whose
expected utility equals the threshold exactly could otherwise be
rejected by rounding, which would break the exactness of
Theorem~\ref{thm:correct} in practice even though it holds in exact
arithmetic.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{Join}: merge a prefix list with an extension-item list}\label{alg:join}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require prefix list $A$, extension list $B$ (item $e$), threshold $\tau$
\State $\rho \gets \min(A.\PTWU,\ B.\PTWU)$
\If{$\rho < \tau - \epsilon$} \Return \textsc{null} \EndIf \Comment{$\PTWU$ before the merge}
\State $eu \gets 0$;\ \ $ub \gets 0$;\ \ $\ell c \gets 0$;\ \ $L \gets$ empty list
\State $i \gets 0$;\ \ $j \gets 0$
\While{$i<|A|$ \textbf{and} $j<|B|$}
  \If{$A.tid_i = B.tid_j$}
     \State $u \gets A.u_i + B.u_j$;\ \ $r \gets \min(A.r_i,\,B.r_j)$;\ \ $\ell p \gets A.\ell p_i + B.\ell p_j$
     \State append $(A.tid_i,\ u,\ r,\ \ell p)$ to $L$
     \State $eu \gets eu + u\cdot e^{\ell p}$
     \If{$u+r>0$} \State $ub \gets ub + e^{\ell p}\,(u+r)$ \EndIf
     \If{$\ell p \ge -\epsilon$} \Comment{$\mathrm P\approx1$: $\EP$ saturates at $1$}
        \State $\ell c \gets \textsc{LogZero}$
     \ElsIf{$\ell c > \textsc{LogZero}$}
        \State $\ell c \gets \max\bigl(\textsc{LogZero},\ \ell c + \log(1-e^{\ell p})\bigr)$
     \EndIf
     \State $i \gets i+1$;\ \ $j \gets j+1$
  \ElsIf{$A.tid_i < B.tid_j$}
     \State $i \gets i+1$
  \Else
     \State $j \gets j+1$
  \EndIf
\EndWhile
\If{$L$ is empty} \Return \textsc{null} \EndIf
\State $q \gets (\ell c \le \textsc{LogZero})\ ?\ 1\ :\ 1-e^{\ell c}$
\State \Return list for $A\cup\{e\}$ with $\EU{=}eu$, $\EP{=}q$, $\PTWU{=}\rho$, $\PUB{=}ub$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The existential probability is accumulated as
$\ell c=\sum_t\log\bigl(1-\mathrm P(Z,t)\bigr)$ rather than as a product,
because the product underflows on lists of realistic length. Two guards
are needed. An entry with $\mathrm P=1$ makes the logarithm diverge, so
it is treated directly: a single certain occurrence already gives
$\EP=1$, and the accumulator saturates at a floor \textsc{LogZero}
($-700$, below which $e^{\ell c}$ is indistinguishable from $0$ in double
precision) and stops accumulating. This branch is exercised by the example of
Section~\ref{subsec:example} and by the certain-data base case, where
every probability equals~$1$; in the benchmark datasets the existence
probabilities lie strictly below~$1$, so there it is never taken. For $\mathrm P<\tfrac12$ the term is
evaluated with $\log 1p$ to retain precision.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{TryCollect}: admit a candidate into the shared top-$\Kk$}\label{alg:collect}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require candidate $J$; min-heap $H$ ordered by $\EU$; size $\Kk$; volatile threshold $\tau$
\If{$J.\EU < \tau$} \Return \EndIf \Comment{lock-free fast reject}
\State \textbf{acquire lock}
\If{$J.\EU \ge \tau$}
  \State $H.\textsc{push}(J)$
  \If{$|H| > \Kk$} $H.\textsc{popMin}()$ \EndIf
  \State $\tau \gets (|H| < \Kk)\ ?\ -\infty\ :\ H.\textsc{min}().\EU$
\EndIf
\State \textbf{release lock}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:collect} is the only structure shared between workers
in three of the four strategies. A candidate is first compared with the
volatile threshold $\tau$ without a lock, which rejects almost every
candidate cheaply; only a candidate that passes takes the lock, is pushed
onto the heap, evicts the weakest member if the heap exceeds $\Kk$, and
republishes $\tau$ as the new $\Kk$-th best $\EU$ (or $-\infty$ while the
heap is not full). In fixed-threshold mode (Problem~\ref{prob:minutil}) the
eviction and the update of $\tau$ are omitted and the heap is unbounded, so
$\tau=\mu$ never moves.

\subsection{Parallelisation Strategies}\label{subsec:parallel}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_strategies.pdf}
\caption{The shared-memory design space. The four parallel strategies are placed
by whether they share the global admission threshold $\tau$ (rows) and how they
balance prefix tasks (columns). SEQ is the single-thread baseline, shown for
reference.}\label{fig:strategies}
\end{figure}
The \emph{baseline} SEQ is a single-thread depth-first pattern-growth miner over
all $1$-itemset prefixes: it follows the utility-list pattern-growth paradigm of
HUI-Miner~\cite{liu2012huiminer} with the single-pass top-$\Kk$ threshold-raising
of TKO~\cite{tseng2016topk}, extended to negative profits in the manner of
TopHUI~\cite{gan2020tophui} and to uncertain data following
PHUIM~\cite{lin2016phuim,nguyen2025dynamic}. SEQ is the exact sequential
reference and the work/time baseline against which the parallel strategies are
measured.

The four strategies are \emph{alternatives}, not components of one
pipeline: each run of the miner uses exactly one of them for Phase~3, and
they are never combined. They are compared on identical input and must
return identical answers.

Building on this core, the \emph{four} parallel strategies span two axes---whether
the admission threshold is \emph{shared}, and how prefix tasks are
\emph{balanced} (Figure~\ref{fig:strategies}):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{FJ} --- fork--join: the prefix range is recursively halved
into tasks on a work-stealing pool, which balances load dynamically; one
shared collector, so any worker's discovery raises $\tau$ for all.
\item \textbf{TPB} --- thread-pool: prefixes are distributed round-robin
over the $\PTWU$-descending order to a fixed pool; one \emph{shared}
collector; static assignment, no stealing.
\item \textbf{PLM} --- partition--local merge: the \emph{same}
round-robin distribution as TPB, but each thread mines into a
\emph{thread-local} collector and the locals are merged at the end; no
threshold sharing during mining.
\item \textbf{PC} --- producer--consumer: a producer enqueues prefix
tasks on a bounded queue and consumers take them as they fall idle,
which balances load dynamically; one shared collector.
\end{itemize}
 
The placement of the four strategies on the two axes
(Figure~\ref{fig:strategies}) is therefore: FJ and PC balance load
dynamically, TPB and PLM statically; FJ, TPB and PC share the threshold,
PLM does not. TPB and PLM deserve particular attention, because they use
an identical prefix distribution and an identical growth procedure and
differ in exactly one respect---whether the collector is shared. The
gap between them is thus a controlled measurement of what threshold
sharing is worth, with load balancing held fixed. FJ and PC differ from
them on the load-balancing axis and from each other in how the balancing
is achieved: FJ decides the split in advance and relies on stealing to
correct it, PC makes no decision at all and lets demand drive the
assignment. By Remark~\ref{rem:sharing}, FJ, TPB and PC prune against
the global $\Kk$-th best, whereas PLM prunes against weaker local
thresholds.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{ForkJoinMine} (FJ): recursive prefix-batch scheduling}\label{alg:fj}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require prefix range $[lo,hi)$, ordered items $\sigma$, lists $\mathcal{L}$, \emph{shared} collector $C$, $\minprob$
\If{$hi - lo \le 1$}
  \For{$p \gets lo$ \textbf{to} $hi-1$}
     \State \textsc{Grow}($\mathcal{L}[\sigma[p]],\ p{+}1,\ \sigma,\ \mathcal{L},\ C,\ \minprob$)
  \EndFor
  \State \Return
\EndIf
\State $m \gets \lfloor (lo+hi)/2 \rfloor$
\State \textbf{fork} \textsc{ForkJoinMine}($[lo,m),\dots$) \Comment{stolen by an idle worker}
\State \textsc{ForkJoinMine}($[m,hi),\dots$) \Comment{run on the current worker}
\State \textbf{join} the forked task
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:fj} turns the prefix range into a binary tree of
tasks. A task covering a single prefix runs \textsc{Grow} on it; a larger
task forks its left half, which an idle worker may steal, and computes its
right half itself. Load balancing is therefore dynamic, but the order in
which prefixes are visited is a by-product of the decomposition: with one
worker the forked halves are drained last-in-first-out, so prefixes are
visited in reverse index order (Section~\ref{subsec:seqrev}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{ThreadPoolMine} (TPB): static round-robin, shared threshold}\label{alg:tpb}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require ordered items $\sigma$, lists $\mathcal{L}$, \emph{shared} collector $C$, $\minprob$, threads $B$
\For{$b \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $B-1$} $\Pi_b \gets \{\,i : 0\le i<|\sigma|,\ i \bmod B = b\,\}$ \EndFor
\For{$b \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $B-1$ \textbf{in parallel}}
  \For{$p \in \Pi_b$ \textbf{in increasing order}}
     \State \textsc{Grow}($\mathcal{L}[\sigma[p]],\ p{+}1,\ \sigma,\ \mathcal{L},\ C,\ \minprob$)
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:tpb} fixes the assignment before mining starts:
prefix $i$ goes to thread $i \bmod B$, so every thread receives a similar
mixture of expensive (low index) and cheap (high index) prefixes, and each
thread processes its share in increasing order. There is no stealing, so a
thread that finishes early idles, but all threads write into one shared
collector and therefore prune against the global threshold.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{PartitionLocalMerge} (PLM): the same distribution as TPB, without threshold sharing}\label{alg:plm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require ordered items $\sigma$, lists $\mathcal{L}$, global collector $C$ (seeded in Phase~2), size $\Kk$, $\minprob$, threads $B$
\For{$b \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $B-1$} $\Pi_b \gets \{\,i : i \bmod B = b\,\}$ \EndFor \Comment{identical to TPB}
\For{$b \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $B-1$}
  \State $C_b \gets$ new top-$\Kk$ collector
  \State seed $C_b$ with every valid $1$-itemset \Comment{same start as the shared strategies}
\EndFor
\For{$b \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $B-1$ \textbf{in parallel}}
  \For{$p \in \Pi_b$ \textbf{in increasing order}}
     \State \textsc{Grow}($\mathcal{L}[\sigma[p]],\ p{+}1,\ \sigma,\ \mathcal{L},\ C_b,\ \minprob$)
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\For{$b \gets 0$ \textbf{to} $B-1$} $C.\textsc{mergeFrom}(C_b)$ \Comment{deduplicating by itemset} \EndFor
\State \Return $C$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Two steps of Algorithm~\ref{alg:plm} exist to keep the comparison
honest. Seeding each local collector with the valid $1$-itemsets costs
nothing---Phase~2 has already computed them, before the work is
partitioned---and any sensible partition-local implementation would do
it. Without it each thread would begin at $\tau=-\infty$ while the
sharing strategies begin at the $\Kk$-th best $1$-itemset, and the
penalty we attribute to the loss of sharing would partly be a startup
handicap we had imposed ourselves. The merge then has to deduplicate by
itemset, because every local now holds those same $1$-itemsets; a
multiset merge would insert $B$ copies of each and evict genuine
patterns, returning a top-$\Kk$ set that differs from the sequential
reference. Deduplication is in any case the correct semantics: the union
of several top-$\Kk$ sets is a set of itemsets, not a multiset.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textsc{ProducerConsumerMine} (PC): dynamic queue scheduling}\label{alg:pc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require ordered items $\sigma$, lists $\mathcal{L}$, \emph{shared} collector $C$, $\minprob$, threads $N$
\State $Q \gets$ bounded blocking queue
\State \textbf{producer:} enqueue $0,\dots,|\sigma|-1$ into $Q$, then $N$ poison tokens
\For{each of $N$ consumers \textbf{in parallel}}
  \Loop
     \State $p \gets Q.\textsc{take}()$
     \If{$p$ is a poison token} \State \textbf{break} \EndIf
     \State \textsc{Grow}($\mathcal{L}[\sigma[p]],\ p{+}1,\ \sigma,\ \mathcal{L},\ C,\ \minprob$)
  \EndLoop
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pc} replaces both the up-front split of FJ and the
static assignment of TPB by demand. A producer places the prefix indices
on a bounded queue in increasing order, followed by one poison token per
consumer; each consumer takes the next index whenever it is idle and mines
it with \textsc{Grow} against the shared collector. An expensive prefix
occupies one consumer while the others continue down the queue, and the
order of consumption is the sequential order, which is why a single
consumer reproduces SEQ exactly.

\subsection{An enumeration-order control}\label{subsec:seqrev}
 
A strategy can do less work than SEQ for two quite different reasons:
because its workers share discoveries, or merely because it happens to
visit the top-level prefixes in a different order. The second effect
needs no parallelism at all, and it is present here by construction.
FJ's task decomposition forks the left half of the prefix range and
computes the right half, so a single work-stealing worker drains its
deque last-in-first-out and visits the top-level prefixes in reverse
index order. Since $\sigma$ is ordered by $\PTWU$ descending, FJ at one
thread mines the \emph{cheap} prefixes first and the expensive ones
last, while SEQ does the opposite. Mining cheap prefixes first raises
$\tau$ before the largest subtree is entered, so the $\PTWU$ and $\PUB$
tests prune far more of it; SEQ pays the full unpruned cost of its most
expensive subtree while $\tau$ is still at its starting value.
 
To separate the two, we add \textbf{SEQ-REV}: SEQ with its top-level
loop run in decreasing index order and nothing else changed. The item
ranking, the suffix bounds, the extension index $j+1$, the three bounds
and the collector are all untouched, so SEQ-REV explores the same search
space and returns the same top-$\Kk$ set as SEQ. It is emphatically
\emph{not} the same as building the UPU-Lists in ascending $\PTWU$
order, which would renumber the items and change which extensions each
prefix is allowed---and hence change the search space itself.
 
SEQ-REV decomposes the difference between SEQ and FJ into three
independent steps:
\[
\underbrace{\text{SEQ}\to\text{SEQ-REV}}_{\text{traversal order}}\quad
\underbrace{\text{SEQ-REV}\to\text{FJ}@1}_{\text{fork--join machinery}}\quad
\underbrace{\text{FJ}@1\to\text{FJ}@B}_{\text{parallelism}}
\]
If SEQ-REV reproduces most of FJ's one-thread advantage, that advantage
belongs to the enumeration order and is available to a purely sequential
miner; if it does not, the advantage really is a property of the task
decomposition, and the control has ruled out the alternative
explanation. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports which of the two holds.
The same control doubles as a drift detector for the measurements: every
strategy at one thread performs exactly the work SEQ or SEQ-REV
performs, so a one-thread run that differs in \emph{time} while matching
in \emph{joins} indicates a change in machine conditions rather than in
the algorithm.


\subsection{Complexity}\label{subsec:complexity}
 
Let $n=|\DB|$, let $N=\sum_{T\in\DB}|T|$ be the total number of
item occurrences, let $\ell=\max_T|T|$ and let $m=|I|$.
 
\paragraph{Phase 1.} Two passes over $\DB$ compute $\PTU$, $\PTWU$ and
the per-item $\EP$ complements in $O(N)$ time. Ranking the surviving
items costs $O(m\log m)$. Building the lists sorts each transaction by
item rank and forms its positive suffix sums; transactions are short, so
this is done with insertion sort at $O(\ell^{2})$ per transaction, giving
$O(n\ell^{2}+N)$ overall. Phase~1 therefore costs
$O(N+n\ell^{2}+m\log m)$ and is linear in the database for the
transaction lengths that occur in practice.
 
\paragraph{Phase 2.} Admitting the valid $1$-itemsets costs
$O(m\log \Kk)$.
 
\paragraph{Phase 3.} A node of the set-enumeration tree is one itemset,
so in the worst case---no pruning---the tree has $2^{m}-1$ nodes and the
problem is exponential, as it is for every exact HUIM method. The
meaningful statement is a cost per unit of work actually done. A
\textsc{Join} of lists of lengths $|A|$ and $|B|$ is a single linear
merge, $O(|A|+|B|)$, and both are at most $n$. Writing $J$ for the
number of joins attempted during a run, Phase~3 costs
$O\bigl(J\cdot\bar{n}\bigr)$ where $\bar{n}$ is the mean list length,
plus $O(J)$ for the pre-merge $\PTWU$ tests that return without merging.
Because the three bounds and the item order are identical across all
strategies, $\bar{n}$ is a property of the data and not of the strategy;
the strategies differ only in $J$. This is precisely why the work ratio
$J/J_{\text{SEQ}}$ is the right instrument for comparing them, and why
it can be read as the share of the dominant cost term that a strategy
avoids or wastes.
 
\paragraph{Space.} A UPU-List of an itemset occupying $c$ transactions
holds four arrays of length $c\le n$. A depth-first path holds at most
one joined list per level and the recursion is at most $m$ deep, so a
worker needs $O(mn)$ in the worst case and far less in practice, since
lists shrink rapidly with itemset size. With $B$ workers the mining
space is $O(Bmn)$; the $1$-itemset lists, $O(N)$ in total, are shared
read-only. PLM adds $B$ collectors of $\Kk$ patterns, $O(B\Kk)$, and PC a
bounded queue of $O(m)$ prefix indices.
 
\paragraph{Synchronisation.} \textsc{TryCollect} reads the volatile
threshold without a lock and acquires one only for a candidate that
passes that read, so the number of lock acquisitions is bounded by the
number of candidates that were plausibly admissible at the moment they
were tested---in practice a small fraction of $J$. PLM acquires no lock
during mining at all, and $B$ at the merge.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
All algorithms are implemented in Java and share one mining core, so
differences in performance isolate the parallelisation strategy. The
experimental harness, the dataset generator and the analysis scripts are
part of the implementation described in the Code availability statement.
 
\paragraph{What ``probabilistic'' means in the data.}
A transaction in the experiments is a list of entries
\texttt{item:quantity:probability}, for example \texttt{1:4:0.61}: item~1
was bought four times, and the database asserts that this entry is present
with probability $0.61$, independently of the other entries
(Definition~\ref{def:db}). The algorithm therefore never sees a single
certain database but a distribution over $2^{N}$ possible worlds, and
$\EU$ and $\EP$ are the expectation and the existence probability of an
itemset under that distribution. The profit table is a separate file of
lines \texttt{item profit}, some of which are negative.

\paragraph{Datasets.}
We use nine benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}), obtained as raw
transaction files from the SPMF repository~\cite{fournierviger2014spmf}.
Their density, the mean transaction length as a percentage of the number
of items, ranges from $0.03\%$ (Kosarak) to $49.3\%$ (Chess), so the
suite covers both extremes of the density axis along which the strategies
are expected to separate. Chess, Mushroom, c20d10k and Accidents are
dense, with long transactions and few items; Kosarak, Retail, Liquor,
T10I4D100K and t25i10d10k are sparse or moderately sparse, with many items and
short transactions.


Two of the nine files are subsets. Kosarak is used as $100{,}000$ of its
$990{,}002$ transactions and Accidents as $20{,}000$ of its $340{,}183$,
because the full files exceed our time budget at the smallest $\Kk$.
Sampling reduces the cost of these two proportionally rather than
changing the shape of the problem: on Accidents, going from
$50{,}000$ to $20{,}000$ transactions---a factor of $2.5$---reduced the
sequential running time by a factor of $2.7$, so the cost is close to
linear in the transaction count over the range we sampled.
 
A tenth dataset, Pumsb ($49{,}046$ transactions, $2{,}113$ items, mean
transaction length $74$), was probed and is reported here as not
completing. No strategy finishes it within a $600$-second limit at
$\Kk=10$, and sampling it to $30\%$ of its transactions does not help,
because its cost is governed by transaction length and density rather
than by transaction count---the mechanism that makes sampling effective
on Accidents is absent. We report this rather than omitting the dataset:
it is a limit of the present exact core, not of the parallelisation
strategies, all four of which fail on it identically, and it is the
clearest motivation for the stronger pruning structure proposed as future
work in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\paragraph{Utility model.}
Benchmark files carry no profits, quantities or probabilities, so these
are generated, and the generator is deterministic: it is driven by a
single seed and applies the same protocol to every dataset except Liquor.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Quantity:} $\qty(i,T)$ is drawn uniformly from $\{1,\dots,5\}$.
\item \emph{Probability:} $\prob(i,T)=\mathrm{round}(U(0.01,0.80),2)$, so
      probabilities lie strictly below $1$ and the certain-entry branch of
      Algorithm~\ref{alg:join} is never taken.
\item \emph{Profit magnitude:} $|\pr(i)|$ is resampled from the pooled
      empirical distribution of the profit magnitudes of the Chess,
      Mushroom and Retail profit tables. The same pooled reference is
      used for every generated dataset, which is why their mean positive
      profits agree to within $15\%$ in Table~\ref{tab:parameters}
      despite describing eight different item universes.
\item \emph{Sign:} about $30\%$ of the items ($29.3$--$30.3\%$ across
      the datasets), chosen uniformly at random, are given a negative profit. The sign is therefore independent of
      the magnitude, which is why the mean positive and negative profits
      in Table~\ref{tab:parameters} are nearly symmetric.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Parameters.}
The range of $\Kk$ is chosen per dataset from a sequential probe so that
the running time grows by at least about a factor of two across the five
values; on the sparse synthetic datasets a range such as $10$--$30$
would leave the time almost flat, and the reported speedups would then
describe the fixed loading cost rather than the mining work. Dense
datasets need smaller $\Kk$ to remain tractable:
$\Kk\in\{10,20,30,40,50\}$ for Chess and c20d10k,
$\{30,\dots,70\}$ for Mushroom,
$\{10,15,20,25,30\}$ for Accidents and Kosarak,
$\{500,\dots,900\}$ for Liquor and Retail, and
$\{500,875,1250,1625,2000\}$ for T10I4D100K and t25i10d10k.
We set $\minprob=0.7$ throughout. SEQ and SEQ-REV are sequential; each
parallel strategy is run with $1$, $2$, $4$ and $8$ threads. Running a
strategy on one thread separates the cost of its machinery from the
benefit of parallelism.
 
\paragraph{Metrics.}
For each configuration we report the wall-clock time of the whole mining
run, speedup, and the \emph{work ratio}: the number of
list joins performed, divided by that of SEQ at the same
$(\text{dataset},\Kk,\minprob)$. The work ratio separates algorithmic effects
---how much of the search tree is explored, which threshold sharing and
enumeration order both change---from hardware parallelism. Because
speedups and work ratios are ratios, they are aggregated over $\Kk$ by
the geometric mean, which is symmetric under inversion; an arithmetic mean
would report $2.1\times$ for a strategy that is four times faster at one
$\Kk$ and four times slower at another. Every parallel strategy is
compared with the sequential baseline of its \emph{own} $(\text{dataset},\Kk)$
cell, never with one timed in a different session.
Since SEQ-REV (Section~\ref{subsec:seqrev}) can be faster than SEQ, we
also report speedup relative to the faster of the two sequential
baselines, which is the speedup a practitioner could have obtained without
any parallelism.
 
\paragraph{Hardware and software.}
All experiments were run on a laptop with a 12th-generation Intel Core i7-12700H
processor: a hybrid design with $6$ performance cores (each two-way simultaneously
multithreaded) and $8$ efficiency cores, for $14$ physical cores and $20$ hardware
threads in total, at a $2.70$\,GHz base frequency. The machine has $16$\,GB of RAM
($15.7$\,GB usable) and runs $64$-bit Windows~11. The implementation targets the Java Virtual Machine (OpenJDK~25,
Eclipse Temurin, HotSpot 64-bit Server VM); no GPU was used. With at most eight
software threads the schedulers use the performance cores first; the hybrid
topology and laptop thermal envelope are discussed as threats to validity
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
 
\paragraph{Protocol.}
Each $(\text{algorithm},\Kk,\minprob,\text{threads})$ configuration is run
after at least one discarded warm-up repetition, so that the just-in-time
compiler has reached steady state before any time is recorded, and the
harness reports the mean and the sample standard deviation of the timed
repetitions. Retail and Accidents use three timed repetitions per
configuration, because a single repetition takes up to about a minute;
all other datasets use five.

All strategies are exact: in every configuration their top-$\Kk$ set was
compared with SEQ's, by itemset and by $\EU$ to six decimals, and no
disagreement occurred.
 
Two safeguards protect the timings. First, every strategy on one thread
performs exactly the work of SEQ (TPB, PLM and PC) or SEQ-REV (FJ), so
their join counts must coincide and their times must agree; a cell where
they do not is one in which the machine changed speed during the
measurement, and the speedups in it are divided by a baseline taken under
different conditions. Such cells are detected automatically, re-run and
replaced, and in the final data every cell passes this control. Second,
the work-stealing strategy FJ is not deterministic: which worker finds a
good itemset first changes the threshold's trajectory, so its join count
and its time vary between repetitions, with coefficients of variation that exceed $10\%$ in $48$ of the $810$
measured configurations and reach $36\%$, while SEQ and SEQ-REV stay at
or below $6\%$. We therefore report the standard deviation alongside every mean
and treat differences between strategies smaller than that spread as
inconclusive.

Across all $810$ measured configurations the median coefficient of
variation is $0.57\%$ and the mean $2.03\%$, so the reported means are
stable at the repetition counts used; the $48$ configurations that exceed
$10\%$ are all fork--join rows, for the reason discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
 
Every configuration returned the same top-$\Kk$ set as SEQ. Of the
$810$ measured configurations, $45$ are the sequential baseline cells
themselves; each of the remaining $765$---the four parallel strategies
at four thread counts and the reverse-order control---was compared with
the SEQ cell of its own $(\text{dataset},\Kk)$ by itemset and by $\EU$
to six decimals, and no disagreement occurred. No configuration in the
nine-dataset suite exceeded the timeout. The
strategies therefore differ only in how long they take and how much of
the search tree they visit, which is what the rest of this section
examines.
 
We report four things in turn: how the strategies scale with the thread
count; where the work they save or waste actually comes from; what
sharing the admission threshold is worth once that question is isolated;
and which strategy a practitioner should choose.

Three observations follow immediately. First, super-linear speedups do
occur, and they are confined to one strategy and explained by something
other than parallelism. Fork--join on Chess at $\Kk=50$ reaches

$2.25\times$ and $3.64\times$ on two, four and eight threads---
sub-linear throughout. The $9.11\times$ is the product of a
$2.50\times$ enumeration-order advantage, available to a purely
sequential miner, and a $3.64\times$ parallel speedup. Across the whole

$36$ strategy--dataset pairs at eight threads is $3.4\times$. A previous
version of this work reported a super-linear speedup as a property of
the parallelisation; the measurement was not what was wrong with it, the
attribution was, and Section~\ref{subsec:order} makes the attribution
measurable.

non-sharing strategy flattens early. PC reaches between $3.3\times$ and
$6.6\times$ at eight threads depending on the dataset; PLM reaches

because each additional partition is one more worker pruning against a
threshold of its own.

$1.00$. TPB, PC and PLM sit at $0.99$--$1.01$ everywhere, as they must:
on one thread each performs exactly SEQ's traversal, so any deviation
would indicate a measurement problem rather than an algorithmic one, and
this is the control described in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. FJ, by
contrast, ranges from $0.53$ on Kosarak to $1.96$ on Chess. A strategy
that has not yet been given a second thread cannot have benefited from
parallelism, so whatever produces that spread is not parallelism. The
next subsection identifies it.

exact: at every $(\text{dataset},\Kk)$ cell, FJ on one thread attempts

resolution of the measurement it \emph{is} the sequential control. The
work-stealing machinery contributes nothing that the reversed loop does
not already contribute, and the entire spread of FJ's one-thread column
in Table~\ref{tab:scaling}---from $0.53$ to $1.96$---is an artefact of
which order its task decomposition happens to impose.
 
Column 5 extends the point to the parallel case. FJ's work ratio at
eight threads tracks SEQ-REV's at one across the whole range, from
$0.603$ against $0.53$ on Chess to $2.251$ against $2.34$ on Kosarak.

hardly at all. Whatever FJ gains from eight threads it gains from
hardware, not from its workers informing one another.
 
The reversed order is not an improvement to be adopted. It is a gamble.
It reduces work on three datasets (Chess, T10I4D100K, Accidents), leaves
it unchanged on two (t25i10d10k, Mushroom), and increases it on four,
reaching $2.34\times$ the sequential work on Kosarak. FJ inherits the
loss exactly as readily as the win, which is why its speedup on Kosarak
is $2.01\times$ while a strategy that commits to no particular order
reaches $5.10\times$ on the same data.
 

This also explains the super-linear figure reported previously, and what
survives of it. That measurement was taken on Chess, the dataset where
the reversed order helps most, with an experimental harness that did not
discard warm-up repetitions, so a sequential baseline inflated by
just-in-time compilation was divided into a parallel time that was not.
Of the two compounding causes, the measurement artefact does not survive
a steady-state baseline: it is gone from the present data. The order
effect does survive, and with it one super-linear cell---fork--join on
Chess at $\Kk=50$, at $9.11\times$ on eight threads. What the control
changes is not the number but what may be concluded from it. That cell
is $2.50\times$ faster than SEQ on a single thread, so the factor by
which its eight workers improve on its own sequential execution is
$3.64\times$; the excess over eight belongs to an enumeration order
that any sequential miner can adopt, and on five of the nine datasets
would do better to avoid.

Sharing pays on every dataset, by a factor between $1.12\times$ and

the partition-local pattern that PLM embodies is the one used by the
distributed frameworks of Section~\ref{sec:related}, which cannot
maintain an exact shared threshold across nodes and therefore pay this
cost by construction.
 
The size of the saving is not predicted by density. The rows of
Table~\ref{tab:sharing} are ordered by density and the saving does not
follow: T10I4D100K, at $1.16\%$ density, saves as much as Chess at
$49.33\%$, while Retail and Liquor, the two sparsest datasets after Kosarak, save
the least ($1.12\times$ and $1.45\times$) and Kosarak itself---sparser
than both---saves $1.93\times$. Whatever governs the magnitude, the structural
statistics in Table~\ref{tab:datasets} do not capture it.

\subsection{Choosing a strategy}\label{subsec:choice}
 
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives time, speedup and work ratio for all four
strategies at eight threads.
 
PC is the best strategy on seven of the nine datasets and is within
$3.5\%$ of the best on an eighth (Mushroom). Its work ratio stays within $0.81$--$1.18$ everywhere, and the width of
that band is the point: PC imposes no traversal order of its own---its
consumers take prefixes in sequential order---so it neither gains nor
pays the order gamble, and what remains below one on Chess
($0.809$) and Accidents ($0.883$) is threshold sharing acting alone. It is, in the sense
R2 asked us to make precise, the \emph{robust} choice: not the fastest
in every case, but the one whose worst case is mildest, and the only
strategy whose behaviour does not depend on a property of the data that
cannot be read off in advance.
 
Chess is the single dataset where another strategy wins by a margin that
matters: FJ reaches $7.17\times$ against PC's $5.98\times$. That is
exactly the dataset where the reversed enumeration order helps most
(Table~\ref{tab:order}, work ratio $0.53$). On Mushroom FJ wins by
$3.5\%$, which is inside the run-to-run spread of FJ itself and should
not be read as a difference.
 
TPB trails PC consistently but never badly, by $4$--$27\%$. The two share
the threshold and differ only in load balancing, so the gap measures what
demand-driven assignment is worth over a static round-robin: real, but an
order of magnitude smaller than what sharing the threshold is worth.
 
PLM is the weakest strategy on seven of the nine datasets, reaching
$1.64$--$3.27\times$ where producer--consumer reaches
$3.3$--$6.6\times$. It is never slower than sequential execution. We
note this explicitly because an earlier version of this work reported
that it was: in that version the partition-local collectors started
empty while the shared collectors started seeded with the valid
$1$-itemsets, so part of the measured penalty was a handicap imposed by
the implementation rather than by the absence of sharing. With both
started identically (Algorithm~\ref{alg:plm}), the penalty is smaller
and entirely attributable to the mechanism under study.
 
On Kosarak and Retail the weakest strategy is not PLM but FJ
($2.01\times$ against PLM's $2.69\times$, and $2.55\times$ against
$2.66\times$). These are the two datasets on which the reversed
enumeration order costs the most work (Table~\ref{tab:order}), and they
make the paper's central point in its sharpest form: a scheduler that
shares the admission threshold but inherits an unlucky traversal order
can finish behind one that shares nothing and traverses in the
sequential order. Threshold sharing is worth between $1.12\times$ and
$3.01\times$ of the exploration; the order is worth between
$0.53\times$ and $2.34\times$ in either direction, and when it goes
against a scheduler it can outweigh everything sharing buys.

\subsection{Base-case validation against an external exact miner}
\label{subsec:basecase}
 
The experiments above compare our parallel strategies against our own
sequential baseline. That establishes internal consistency but not
external correctness: if the shared core were wrong, every strategy
would be wrong in the same way and the comparison would not reveal it.
This subsection closes that gap, and at the same time quantifies what
the generality of the PPNHUI model costs relative to a specialised
classical miner.
 
\paragraph{Reduction to the classical case.}
Our problem degenerates to classical HUIM under two restrictions. If no
item carries a negative profit, then $\uu(\itemset,T)\ge 0$ for every
transaction, and if every existence probability equals $1$, then
$\mathrm{P}(\itemset,T)=1$ for all $\itemset\subseteq T$. Substituting
into Definition~\ref{def:prob} gives $\EU(\itemset)=\sum_{T\supseteq
\itemset}\uu(\itemset,T)$, the ordinary utility, and $\EP(\itemset)=1$
for every non-empty itemset, so the $\EP$ filter never binds.
Equation~\eqref{eq:ptwurem} reduces $\PTWU$ to the classical
transaction-weighted utilisation and Eq.~\eqref{eq:pub} reduces $\PUB$
to the remaining-utility bound of HUI-Miner~\cite{liu2012huiminer}. The
mining core is unchanged; only the data makes the probabilistic and
negative-profit machinery inert. We additionally replace the top-$\Kk$
collector by the fixed admission threshold of
Problem~\ref{prob:minutil}, which Theorem~\ref{thm:correct} already
covers as the case $\tau=\mu$. In this configuration the problem solved
is exactly the one EFIM solves, so the two programs must return the same
answer.
 
\paragraph{Data and protocol.}
We derive two base-case datasets from the benchmarks of
Table~\ref{tab:datasets}: \textsc{RetailBase} ($88{,}162$ transactions,
total utility $46{,}001{,}038$) and \textsc{T10Base} ($100{,}000$
transactions, $870$ distinct items, total utility $53{,}783{,}150$),
generated with all profits positive and all existence probabilities set
to~$1$. Each is converted to the SPMF utility format and the conversion
is verified by round-trip: every transaction is re-parsed and every
declared transaction utility is re-derived from its per-item utilities.
Five minimum-utility thresholds per dataset were chosen to span roughly
an order of magnitude in the number of answers. Because each SPMF
invocation starts a fresh JVM, we ran our miner the same way---three
separate single-run JVM invocations per threshold rather than three
timed runs inside one JVM---so that neither side benefits from a
just-in-time warm-up the other does not get, and we report medians. This
differs deliberately from the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:setup},
where discarded warm-up repetitions are the correct choice because every
strategy being compared runs on the same JVM; here the comparison
crosses process boundaries, and symmetry between the two programs
matters more than steady state. The protocol is not cosmetic: on
\textsc{RetailBase} the first invocation was consistently $11\%$ slower
than the second and third, an operating-system page-cache effect that a
single run would have silently reported as the result.
 
\paragraph{Correctness.}
At the lowest and highest threshold of each dataset we compared the two
outputs itemset by itemset. All four comparisons agree exactly: the same
number of itemsets ($1013$ and $99$ on \textsc{RetailBase}; $1011$ and
$98$ on \textsc{T10Base}), the same itemsets, and the same utility for
every itemset to within floating-point tolerance. No itemset was found
by one program and missed by the other. Agreement with an independently
written exact miner is a considerably stronger correctness statement
than agreement with our own sequential baseline, because the two
implementations share no code, no data structure and no author.
 
\paragraph{The cost of generality.}
Table~\ref{tab:basecase} reports the comparison. Our miner is faster at
every threshold on both datasets, by $1.5$--$6.9\times$ on
\textsc{RetailBase} and $7.4$--$16.6\times$ on \textsc{T10Base}, using
one thread against EFIM's one thread. The cost of carrying the
probabilistic and positive--negative machinery through the base case is
therefore not merely small but negative: the generalised core is faster
than a specialised classical miner even on the classical miner's own
problem.
 
The shape of the comparison is more informative than its magnitude.
EFIM's runtime is almost flat in the threshold---on \textsc{T10Base} it
varies by $3\%$ across a range over which the number of answers falls by
a factor of ten---whereas ours falls by $2.2\times$ on the same range.
EFIM projects and rescans the database at each node of its search, so
its cost is dominated by a term that the threshold barely influences;
the UPU-List pays its scanning cost once, during Phase~1, after which
pruning translates directly into work not done. The advantage is thus
structural rather than a constant factor of implementation, and it
widens as the threshold rises.

\paragraph{What the base case reveals about top-$\Kk$.}
\label{par:minutilworkratio}
Running the parallel strategies in this fixed-threshold mode produces a
result that bears directly on the scope of this paper's claims. Across
every configuration of both base-case datasets---five thresholds,
$\{1,2,4,8\}$ threads, SEQ-REV, PC and PLM---the work ratio is
\emph{exactly} $1.000$. Neither reversing the traversal order nor
withholding the global threshold from the workers changes the number of
joins by a single one.
 
This is the expected behaviour, and it is worth stating explicitly
because it delimits the contribution. Both mechanisms studied in this
paper act through the \emph{admission threshold}. When $\tau$ is supplied
in advance, sharing it is vacuous---every worker already holds the final
value from the outset---and enumeration order cannot matter either,
because whether a node is pruned depends only on its own bounds against
a constant, not on what has been discovered before it. PLM's penalty and
fork--join's order effect therefore both vanish, leaving only raw
parallelism: on \textsc{T10Base} at eight threads PC reaches
$5.5$--$5.9\times$ and PLM $4.8$--$5.3\times$, the gap between them now
attributable solely to PC's dynamic queue against PLM's static
partitioning and end-of-run merge.
 
The two effects analysed in Sections~\ref{sec:results}
and~\ref{sec:discussion}---what threshold sharing saves, and what
enumeration order costs or saves---are therefore specific to top-$\Kk$
mining rather than general properties of parallel HUIM. A practitioner
mining at a supplied threshold should choose a strategy on
load-balancing grounds alone, and for that reader the work-ratio metric
carries no information at all: it is identically one.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
 
\paragraph{Two mechanisms, only one of them dependable.}
The study separates two ways in which a parallel top-$\Kk$ miner can
explore less of the tree than a sequential one. Sharing the admission
threshold lets a discovery by one worker prune another's branch, and it
saves between $1.12\times$ and $3.01\times$ of the work on every dataset
we measured. Visiting the top-level prefixes in a different order raises
the threshold earlier or later, and it changes the work by a factor
between $0.53\times$ and $2.34\times$---in either direction. The first
mechanism is a property of the algorithm; the second is a property of
the data, and a scheduler that fixes a traversal order is betting on it.
 
Fork--join makes that bet without meaning to. Its order is not a design
choice but a by-product of forking the left half of a range and
computing the right, and Table~\ref{tab:order} shows that the bet
accounts for all of its pruning behaviour. A reader who sees fork--join
do less work than the baseline and concludes that work stealing enabled
cooperation would be drawing the wrong lesson from the right number.
 
\paragraph{Density does not predict the regime.}
An earlier version of this work organised its findings around a
density-driven regime spectrum. The nine-dataset suite refutes that
organisation, and does so by a matched pair rather than by a weak
correlation. c20d10k and Accidents have densities of $10.42\%$ and
$10.09\%$---as close as any two datasets in the suite---yet the reversed
enumeration order costs $1.55\times$ the work on the first and saves
$0.79\times$ on the second. Nor does density order the value of
threshold sharing (Table~\ref{tab:sharing}). Density is a reasonable
first guess, because dense data tends to produce a few dominant itemsets
early, but it is not the governing variable, and no other statistic in
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} orders the outcomes either.
 
\paragraph{What a practitioner can do instead.}
The control that resolves the attribution question also answers the
practical one, because it is cheap. One sequential run of SEQ-REV
alongside SEQ on a sample measures the order effect directly. Where that
ratio is well below one---$0.53$ on Chess---a work-stealing scheduler
converts it into the largest speedup available. Where it is near or
above one, the order is a liability and a strategy that imposes none is
the better choice. We state this as a diagnostic rather than a rule,
because the diagnostic is not sufficient on its own: on Accidents the
order helps ($0.79$) and fork--join still loses to producer--consumer by
$24\%$, since the variance and scheduling overhead of work stealing cost
more than the saved work returns. The safe reading is narrower and holds
throughout our data: producer--consumer unless the order effect is
large, and then only if the variance of work stealing is acceptable.
 
\paragraph{Run-to-run variance of work stealing.}
FJ is the only strategy whose results are not reproducible run to run.
Which worker first finds a high-utility itemset determines when the
threshold rises, and therefore how much of the tree the others explore,
so its join count varies between repetitions and its time varies with it:
coefficients of variation exceed $10\%$ in $48$ of the $810$ measured
configurations and reach $36\%$, while SEQ, SEQ-REV, TPB, PC and PLM
stay at or below $6\%$. Every configuration our validation flags above
$10\%$ is a fork--join row. The spread is largest at four and eight
threads but is not confined to them: one Kosarak cell at two threads
reaches $31\%$, and a single Chess cell at one thread reaches $21\%$,
the latter being timing noise rather than scheduling nondeterminism,
since at one thread fork--join's traversal is fixed. This is a property
of the execution model and not of the measurement: it persists across
repetitions and does not appear for the statically partitioned or
queue-based strategies at the same thread counts. It is also a reason to
prefer a deterministic strategy in production even where fork--join's
mean is slightly better, as on Mushroom.

The mechanism is directly visible in one cell. On Kosarak at $\Kk=10$
with two threads, four of the five repetitions took about $34{,}100$~ms
while the fifth took $61{,}663$~ms---and that repetition's join count
was $139{,}291{,}808$, exactly the one-thread count. On that run the
work-stealing pool never stole, so a two-thread execution performed a
one-thread traversal.
 
\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
The utility model is generated rather than measured for eight of the
nine datasets: quantities, existence probabilities and profit signs come
from the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:setup}, so the absolute runtimes
depend on choices we made. Liquor is the exception, carrying real
quantities, real probabilities and real average utilities, and it
behaves like its sparse neighbours in every comparison reported here,
which is evidence that the conclusions are not artefacts of the
generator. The hardware is a single hybrid-core laptop CPU: at four
threads the scheduler may place a worker on an efficiency core, which
interacts with work stealing and contributes to FJ's variance, although
the one-thread control shows that FJ's pruning behaviour is already
established before any scheduling across cores occurs. The $\EP$ filter
is not the binding constraint on these datasets---because $\EP$ takes a
product over all transactions containing an itemset, it approaches one
on databases of this size even when individual probabilities are
small---so the suite exercises the density and positive--negative
dimensions more strongly than the probabilistic one. Finally, Pumsb was
probed and did not complete within the timeout at the smallest $\Kk$ even
after sampling; we report it in Section~\ref{sec:setup} as a limit of the
present exact core rather than omitting it.
 
 
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
 
We have studied the design space of shared-memory parallel mining for the
Top-$\Kk$ Probabilistic Positive--Negative High-Utility Itemset problem,
in which top-$\Kk$ selection, uncertain data and negative profits meet. On
a common exact core built on the UPU-List, with three upper bounds proven
valid under negative profits and under both a discovered and a supplied
admission threshold, we instantiated four classical parallelisation
schemes and compared them against each other and against two sequential
baselines.
 
The methodological result is that a work-ratio metric is not sufficient
to attribute a parallel speedup. A scheduler can explore less of the tree
than the baseline for a reason that has nothing to do with parallelism,
and in our data the work-stealing scheduler does exactly that: on one thread it performs exactly the work of a purely sequential
reverse-order control---the same join count, to the digit, on all nine
datasets, and at eight threads its work ratio
still tracks that control. Without the control, its pruning would have
been credited to cooperation between workers; with it, the credit goes to
an enumeration order that a sequential miner can adopt for free---and
that costs more than it saves on four of the nine datasets.
 
The substantive result is what remains once that confound is removed.
Sharing the admission threshold, measured on a pair of strategies that
differ in nothing else, saves between $1.12\times$ and $3.01\times$ of
the explored work on every dataset, dense and sparse alike. It is the
only effect in the study that never changes sign, and it is precisely the
effect that the partition-local pattern used throughout the distributed
parallel HUIM literature cannot obtain. A producer--consumer scheduler,
which shares the threshold and imposes no traversal order of its own, is
the best or joint-best strategy on eight of nine datasets and has the
mildest worst case of the four.
 
We also delimit these findings. Under a supplied utility threshold they
all disappear: the work ratio is exactly $1.000$ in every configuration
measured, because both mechanisms act on a threshold that no longer
moves. The design question answered here is therefore specific to
top-$\Kk$ mining rather than general to parallel utility mining. The core
itself is validated outside our own code, agreeing itemset by itemset
with an independently written published exact miner on a reduced base
case.
 
Several directions remain. An adaptive scheduler could measure the
enumeration-order effect on a sample during Phase~1 and choose its Phase~3
strategy from it, obtaining fork--join's advantage where the order pays
and producer--consumer's robustness where it does not. The core would
benefit from an estimated-utility co-occurrence structure, which prunes
most on exactly the dense data where the present core is slowest and
where Pumsb exceeds our budget. Controlled experiments that hold density
fixed while varying the negative-profit fraction would isolate any
residual effect of negative items, which our suite cannot separate from
density. Finally, a server-class processor with uniform cores would
remove the one hardware effect we could not eliminate, and a distributed
extension that approximates a shared threshold across nodes would test
whether the mechanism this paper measures can be recovered at the scale
where the partition-local pattern currently prevails.
